Does ChatGPT leak your data?

You paste stack traces, config files and customer emails into ChatGPT every day. Here is what actually happens to that text, where the real risks are, and how to keep using AI without handing over anything you would regret.

Short answer: ChatGPT will not post your chats publicly, but everything you paste leaves your device, is stored on OpenAI's servers, and can be exposed by a stolen account, a bug or a training setting you forgot to turn off. The safe habit is simple: never paste real secrets or personal data. Replace them with placeholders first.

What happens to text you paste into ChatGPT

The moment you press Enter, your message is sent to OpenAI and saved with your chat history. From there it can be:

  • Stored for as long as the chat exists, and for a while after you delete it.
  • Used to train future models, unless you have turned that off in your data controls. On business plans it is off by default.
  • Reviewed by people in some cases, for example for safety and abuse monitoring.
  • Read by anyone who gets into your account, including a teammate on a shared login or someone who steals your password.

None of this means OpenAI is careless. It means your paste is now data on a third-party server, with every risk that brings.

Has ChatGPT actually leaked data before?

Yes. The best-known cases were not clever attacks on the model. They were ordinary software bugs and human mistakes:

  • The 2023 chat history bug. A bug in an open-source library briefly showed some users the titles of other people's chats and exposed limited payment details for a small share of subscribers. OpenAI took ChatGPT offline to fix it.
  • The Samsung incident. Engineers pasted internal source code and meeting notes into ChatGPT to get help. Nothing was hacked, but confidential data had left the company, and Samsung restricted AI tools afterwards.
  • Stolen accounts. Security researchers have found large batches of ChatGPT logins for sale, taken by malware on people's computers. Whoever buys one can read the whole chat history.

The pattern is clear. The biggest risk is not the AI remembering your secret and telling a stranger. It is that the secret now exists in one more place that can break.

Do ChatGPT's privacy settings fix it?

They help, but they do not make pasting secrets safe.

  • Turning off model training (Settings → Data controls) stops new chats from being used to improve models. Your chats are still stored.
  • Temporary Chat is not saved to your history or used for training, but it is still sent to OpenAI and kept for a short period for safety checks.
  • Deleting a chat removes it from your view. Removal from the servers is not instant.

Settings limit how far your data travels. They cannot un-send a password you already pasted.

How to use ChatGPT without leaking anything

The AI almost never needs the real values. It needs the shape of your problem. So swap the sensitive parts for placeholders before you paste:

DATABASE_URL=postgres://admin:Sup3rS3cret@db.acme.io:5432/prod
Customer: Jane Miller, jane.miller@gmail.com, +1 415 555 0182

becomes

DATABASE_URL=postgres://[USER_1]:[PASSWORD_1]@[HOST_1]:5432/prod
Customer: [NAME_1], [EMAIL_1], [PHONE_1]

ChatGPT can still debug the connection string or draft the reply to the customer. When the answer comes back, you put the real values back on your side.

  1. Mask secrets: API keys, tokens, passwords, connection strings, private keys.
  2. Mask personal data: names, emails, phone numbers, addresses, IDs, card numbers.
  3. Keep the structure. Use consistent placeholders like [EMAIL_1] so the AI can follow the logic.
  4. Restore locally. Swap the real values back into the answer on your own device, never in the chat.
  5. If you already pasted a real secret, rotate it now. Deleting the chat is not enough.

Is Claude or Gemini more private than ChatGPT?

Each company has its own retention and training rules, and they change often. But every cloud AI receives and stores what you send. Switching assistants changes who holds your data, not whether someone does. The fix that works everywhere is the same: do not send the real secret in the first place.
